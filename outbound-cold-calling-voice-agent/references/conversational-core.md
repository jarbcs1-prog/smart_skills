## Rapport

Rapport is a working relationship built on mutual understanding. It is not charm — charm is unreliable and doesn't survive a busy day. Rapport is what makes a stranger willing to spend two minutes on you.

It matters because people buy from businesses they feel comfortable with and because rapport is what produces information. Someone who feels sold *to* gives short answers. Someone who feels *understood* tells you what's actually wrong.

**In a voice call, rapport is structural, not social.** You can't smile or mirror body language, so rapport comes from: sounding unhurried, asking about their situation rather than your offer, remembering what they said and being brief. Those are behaviors an agent can actually execute.

## The First 20 Seconds

The first 20 seconds decide whether there is a conversation at all. Recipients have learned to filter unknown calls, so the opening must clear three filters simultaneously:
1. **Is this a sales call?** — Answer it before they ask. Ambiguity is read as sales.
2. **Is it legitimate?** — A real company, a real reason, a traceable source.
3. **What does it cost me?** — Seconds or minutes, stated as a number.

A good opening does all three in one breath:
```
"Hi, this is [Name] calling from [Company] about [specific topic] — it's a
quick question, about [n] minutes. Is now a bad time?"
```

**What kills openings:** long self-introductions, apologies for calling, enumeration of the whole company and any hint that the real reason is a sale.

**Variance matters.** Five identical openings across a batch is how people learn the calls are automated. Rotate the surface while keeping the structure.

## Active Listening and Empathy

Active listening is not waiting for your turn. It's demonstrating that you tracked what was actually said.

**Paraphrase before responding.** This is the single highest-value listening move on a call:

```
"So if I'm hearing you right — the issue isn't the price, it's that you can't get a straight answer about when it'll be finished?"
```

It buys three things: it proves you were listening, it lets them correct you before you build on a misunderstanding and it signals that you understood the problem rather than the symptom.

**Empathy is acknowledgment, not agreement.** You can recognize something is genuinely frustrating without agreeing or apologizing for their situation.

```
"I can see why that'd be frustrating — you've had three different answers and still don't know."
```

Then move on. One sentence. Dwell on it and it becomes performed sympathy, which reads as manipulative.

**Never judge or criticise.** Not their choice of supplier, not their process, not their previous bad experience. Criticism of their past decision invites them to defend it, which is the opposite of progress.

## Question Taxonomy

Questions are the primary tool and they do different jobs. Using the wrong one at the wrong time wastes the call.

| Type | Job | Example |
|------|-----|---------|
| **Open** | Elicit detail | "What's happening with that at the moment?" |
| **Probing** | Go deeper on a specific answer | "What happens to it after that?" |
| **Closed** | Confirm or verify | "So you're looking at this before the end of the quarter?" |
| **Outcome-imagining** | Move them toward the decision | "If that was sorted, what would change for you?" |
| **Anchor-uncovering** | Learn their expectation | "Was there a figure in mind for this?" |

### Open-ended

Start with what, how and why. These produce the information you can't get any other way.

**One question per turn.** On a call, two questions means one is lost — people can't rewind audio.

### Probing

When an answer is vague, go one level deeper rather than moving on:

```
"Got it. What happens at that point — does it stop there or does someone chase it up?"
```

Vague answers are usually a surface symptom. One probe usually finds the real constraint and the real constraint is what you can actually sell against.

### Closed

Use for confirmation, not discovery. "Just so I've got that right — you're looking at this before the end of the quarter?" It's cheap, it prevents expensive misunderstanding and it signals attentiveness.

### Outcome-imagining

The most useful question type for moving a conversation forward and the one most agents skip:

```
"If that were dealt with, what would that change for you day to day?"
```

It gets you the *consequence* rather than the *complaint* and consequences are what make a value argument land. It also works for non-commercial offers — "what would it change for you" works for surveys, renewals and follow-ups.

### Anchor-uncovering

People arrive with an expectation. Discovering it early is what stops you quoting into a vacuum — see `pricing-and-positioning.md`.

```
"Were you thinking of a particular range for this?"
```

They're giving you their ceiling. Asking costs nothing.

## Language Mirroring

Use their words, not yours. When they say "the system," say "the system" — even if you'd have called it "the platform." When they say a competitor's name, say that name.

Mirroring signals that you're speaking their language rather than pitching a category they don't shop in. It's the cheapest rapport available and it costs one habit of attention.

**Watch for their vocabulary tier.** If they say "cold calling," don't say "outbound engagement." Technical buyers respond to precision; non-technical buyers disengage from it. See [adapting to the person](#adapting-to-the-person).

## Adapting to the Person

Most agents use one register and hope it works. The same pitch, delivered in the wrong register, fails for reasons that have nothing to do with the offer.

| Read this signal | They're likely | Adjust to |
|------------------|----------------|-----------|
| Short, "yeah", "sure" | Impatient or uninterested | Get to the point fast. One question, then offer an exit |
| Long detailed answers | Engaged, wants to be heard | Ask follow-ups. Don't rush the close |
| Precise technical terms | Sophisticated buyer | Be precise. Vague answers read as unprepared |
| "I dunno", general terms | Non-technical | Plain language. No acronyms |
| Asks about price early | Budget-driven or braced for a pitch | Isolate before quoting — see pricing reference |
| Asks "who are you" | Skeptical | Disclose plainly, then move to their problem |
| Interruptive | Confident, time-poor | Lead with the point. Get to the offer sooner |
| Hedging, apologizing | Low confidence, may need championing | Reassure. Make it easy to say yes |

**Two adjustments maximum.** A caller who changes register more than twice feels manipulated. Read early, adjust once, then let the conversation be.

**On detecting this on a call:** you have tone and pace, which the written sales literature doesn't. Hesitation and filler words suggest uncertainty; long silent pauses kill calls; fast clipped answers suggest impatience. All are inferential — treat them as suggestions to adjust, not facts to act on.

## Storytelling

Stories engage memory in a way feature lists don't. But a story has to earn its place.

**Structure:** situation → tension → resolution. Skip any element that doesn't serve the listener. In a call, a story that runs long is a story that gets interrupted.

**Selection rule:** the story must be about *their* situation, not your success. "Another client doubled their revenue" is about you. "Most people in your position find the bottleneck is [their specific issue]" is about them.

**Length discipline:** roughly the length of two or three sentences. On a cold call, a long story is a signal that you're stalling.

**Vivid beats matter.** "We cut response time from six hours to under two" is memorable. "We improved service levels considerably" is not.

**Never fabricate.** Invented case studies are a compliance issue as well as a credibility one. Use only stories the entity record contains.

## Social Proof

Proof reduces perceived risk. It works best when it is specific, recent and similar to the listener.

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| "We have many happy clients" | "[Comparable type] in [similar situation] saw [specific result]" |
| "Trusted by businesses" | "We work with [named sector] across [region]" |
| Generic testimonials | Testimonial with name, role and the actual outcome |

**Don't oversell.** Three well-matched proofs beat ten. Cramming proof into every call makes it sound like a brochure.

**Match on similarity, not prestige.** A proof from someone the listener recognizes as similar to themselves is worth several from bigger names they feel unlike.

## Credibility and Trust

Trust is built from specific behaviors:
1. **Demonstrate relevant knowledge** — but only where it's asked for. Assumed expertise reads as bluff.
2. **Show track record** — real examples, verifiable.
3. **Be transparent** — including limitations. "This won't work if you need same-day turnaround" is more credible than an unqualified promise.
4. **Have a real guarantee or warranty** — and mean it.
5. **Deliver what you promised** — the follow-through is what makes the call matter.

**On transparency:** volunteering a limitation is the strongest trust move available on a cold call, precisely because nobody else does it. It also filters bad-fit recipients before they waste your time.

**On authority claims:** never imply regulatory approval, accreditation or endorsement that doesn't exist. See `outbound-compliance.md`.

## Common Ground

Finding shared ground makes the conversation feel like a conversation between people rather than a broadcast.

**Before the call:** research from what's legitimately available — company website, public filings, sector, size, recent news. Use one detail, never a staircase of them.

**During:** notice sector, role and any pressure signals ("before the quarter closes", "when the new person starts"). Reflect the pressure back rather than proving you understand their world.

**Never manufacture familiarity.** Stating something you inferred and they never told you — their team size, their revenue, a problem they haven't mentioned — is the single fastest way to lose credibility. If you don't know, ask.

**Listen for their language and reuse it.** That's common ground too.

## Voice-Specific Adaptation

The sales literature is written for text or in-person calls. Voice is neither. Adapting matters more here.

| Literature assumes | On a voice call |
|--------------------|-----------------|
| Bold and bullet points | **Prosody.** Emphasis is a word plus a pause, not formatting |
| Re-read the message | **No re-reading.** Everything is said once |
| Formatting for emphasis | **Pace change.** Slow down to mark importance |
| Silence signals disengagement | **Silence is safe.** Wait; they'll fill it |
| Typo-free precision | **Doubles down on clarity**, since there's no visual fallback |
| Long considered replies | **Latency tolerance is low.** First audio latency decides |

**The core voice adaptation:** in text you can structure with formatting. In voice you can only structure with **sequencing, pacing and pauses**. Say the value first, then the price — never the reverse. Pause before a number. Slow down before the part that matters.

**One question per turn, always.** Text tolerates lists because the reader can scroll back. A caller cannot rewind.
