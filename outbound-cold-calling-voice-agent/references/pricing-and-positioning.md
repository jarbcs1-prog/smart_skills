# Pricing, Value and Anchoring

**Offer-agnostic method for putting a number on a call.** Works for products,
services, subscriptions, contracts and renewals. Nothing here assumes a ticket
size or an industry.

The governing insight: **price is not a number, it's a signal.** The buyer
reads far more into your figure than the digits themselves and treating price
as arithmetic is what puts you in a discount argument you didn't choose.

## Price as a Signal

Buyers use price as a shortcut for quality when they lack expertise to judge
otherwise. Quote well under the market and the internal alarm doesn't ring with
joy — it rings with suspicion. *What's wrong with it? Will they deliver?*

| Signal sent | Signal received |
|-------------|-----------------|
| Very low price | Low quality, hidden costs, desperation |
| Very high price | High quality, high commitment, not desperate |
| Hesitant, apologetic | Low confidence — treat me the same way |
| Stated flat, immediately | Confidence — this is a real number |

Price also drives **commitment**. Buyers who pay a real amount use the product.
Buyers who got it cheap let it gather dust. Underpricing doesn't win the deal;
it makes the deal less likely to succeed.

**Never apologize for a price.** "Sorry, that's higher than you might have
expected" concedes before they've said anything.

## The Pain Audit

You cannot justify a number you haven't anchored against something. The status
quo has a cost; if you don't quantify it, your price is just a number with
nothing to compare it to.

Ask for **components**, never the total:

```
"How many hours a week does the team spend on that?"
"What does the average sale look like when that goes wrong?"
"How often does that happen?"
```

**Three dimensions of loss** — check all three, most people only see one:

| Dimension | What it is | Example |
|-----------|------------|---------|
| **Direct** | Cash actually lost | Shipping, overtime, penalties, refunds |
| **Opportunity** | Revenue not earned | Customers who went elsewhere, capacity idle |
| **Risk** | Downside not yet realized | Fines, churn, failure recovery costs |

Then reverse it — what would the outcome be worth?

```
"If that were properly sorted, what would it mean for you in numbers?"
```

**Use their figures, even round ones.** "Maybe twenty", "couple of hundred" —
take the round number. Precision you invented undermines everything else.

**Do the arithmetic for them.** That's a service, not a tactic. State the result,
ask if it's about right, then stop. Piling on more numbers reads as pressure.

**Ask for a reality check, not agreement on price:**

```
"Does that figure sound about right to you?"
```

If they correct it, you've learned something valuable and avoided building on a
wrong assumption.

## Reference Prices and Anchors

Every buyer carries an internal number they'll measure you against. Three
sources:

| Anchor | What it is | Handling |
|--------|------------|----------|
| **Market** | What they believe it "should" cost | Re-frame the category |
| **Competitor** | An actual quote they hold | Ask for exact scope |
| **History** | What they paid last time | Reset the clock |

**Uncover it before you quote.** Two questions, both of which do the work for you:

```
"Were you thinking of a particular range?"
"Have you looked at other options?"
```

They're giving you their ceiling. A range told to you is a gift; the same range
you guessed wrong is a disaster.

**On competitors:** never match a lower quote immediately. It costs margin *and*
signals your original number was inflated. Instead ask what's in scope —
lower prices are usually reduced scope. Never criticise a competitor personally;
compare scopes.

**On history:** acknowledge it, then re-qualify the problem. "That's what it
cost then, for the version available then, against the problem you had then."
Not defensively — just factually, then return to today's problem.

## The 10% Rule

Once you know their anchor, the gap dictates your response:

| Gap to anchor | Response |
|---------------|----------|
| **≤ 10%** | Justify with specific inclusions, not ROI |
| **20–30%** | Needs a clear ROI calculation |
| **> 50%** | Likely the wrong conversation. Step down your offer or exit and refer |

That last row matters. Recognizing a 3× mismatch early is cheaper than spending
the call justifying it.

## Translating Features into Value

A feature is a fact. A value is a benefit with a currency attached.

Apply **"so what?"** until you reach money or time:

```
"Cloud-based access."
  → So what? You can check inventory anywhere.
    → So what? You approve orders on the road instead of waiting.
      → So what? Two days faster cash cycle, roughly $1,200 released.
```

Four translation routes, by what the buyer cares about:

| Route | Use when | Shape |
|-------|----------|-------|
| **Direct gain** | Revenue/quantity | "Generates [n] more [units]/month" |
| **Time returned** | Labor cost | "Saves 5 hours/week ≈ $[n] at their loaded rate" |
| **Avoided cost** | Insurance/risk/compliance | "Costs $[n] to fix vs $[m] to recover" |
| **Capacity** | Agencies, consultants | "Frees [n] billable hours" |

**Time is the strongest currency in B2B**, because it's unambiguous and
defensible. Two hours a week is a fact; "improved efficiency" is not.

**Don't extrapolate absurdly.** Turning $5 into a 10-year claim reads as a trick.
Use 1–3 year horizons.

## ROI and Payback

```
monthly_benefit = total_quantified_annual_gain/12
payback_months  = total_price/monthly_benefit
```

State it as a fraction of the problem:

```
"Based on what you've told me, that's roughly $[n] a month you're losing. This
is $[price]. It pays for itself in about [m] months — after that it's back in
your pocket."
```

**Order matters on voice:** gain first, then price. "It costs $[price], but it
saves $[gain]" makes the listener anchor on the price. "It saves $[gain] and it
costs $[price]" makes them anchor on the gain. You can't reformat a sentence in
a call, so sequence deliberately.

## Cost-Per-Use

When the total looks large even with good ROI, change the unit instead of the
number. This fights the one-big-expense reflex.

```
"That's $[total] for the year — which is $[monthly] a month. Across [n] [jobs/
appointments/records], that's $[per_use] each. One [unit] costs you $[their
figure], so it pays for itself on the first couple."
```

**This is the single most effective technique for a large number on a cold
call.** A total is abstract; a per-unit figure is comparable to something they
already know.

## Three Ways to Present

Pick based on what the call needs:

| Approach | Use when | Structure |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| **Itemise** | They want detail or have a committee | Line items, each tied to a specific pain they named |
| **Bundle** | They're ready to buy | Named package, single number, inclusions stated |
| **Per-use** | The total feels large | Cost per unit, against a unit cost they gave you |

On a call you rarely send a document, so **say the structure**. "It's three
parts — setup, then monthly, then support" gives them a shape to hold.

**Name components specifically.** "Implementation and data migration" is
defensible; "service fee" is not. Vague line items get challenged because they
don't visibly correspond to anything they asked for.

## Positioning: Premium vs Budget

Choose deliberately, because your price has to match your story.

**Premium** signals confidence and selectivity. It attracts buyers who value
outcome and it filters out the ones who will consume unlimited support at a
discounted rate. Attach price to the outcome, never to your effort. Don't
apologize. Don't pre-discount.

**Budget** signals efficiency and accessibility. It needs active defense, because
the "cheap equals bad" bias is the default assumption. State *why* it's cheaper
as a structural fact — lean team, low overhead, self-serve — so it reads as a
model rather than a compromise.

**The trap in both:** a price far below the market anchor reads as danger unless
you've already established the higher reference. If you're going to come in
under market, anchor the normal range first, then present yours as the
considered option.

## Structuring Options

When there genuinely are options, present three:

| Tier | Role |
|------|------|
| **Entry** | Accessible, but deliberately incomplete. It's the baseline, not the target. |
| **Recommended** | The one you want. Core plus what addresses their stated pain. |
| **Premium** | Makes the middle tier look considered. |

The middle option wins disproportionately because it avoids both the risk of
the cheap tier and the waste of the expensive one. **This is presentation, not
manipulation** — if the entry option genuinely fits them, recommend it.

On voice, don't dump three options into one breath. Name them, recommend one and offer to follow up with detail.

## Scope and Certainty

Ambiguity is what makes buyers delay. Remove it and you justify the price
without arguing.

State aloud: what's included, what isn't and the timeframe. "It's X, which
covers A and B — not C and it takes about [n] weeks."

**Exclusions are the part people skip and shouldn't.** Saying "that doesn't
include third-party fees" prevents the scope-creep conversation three weeks later
and demonstrates you're disciplined.

Certainty is itself valuable. Buyers pay a premium for not having to
micromanage and explicit scope is a large part of that.

## The Discount

Discounting on a call you've just earned is the most expensive concession
available — it implies the first number was inflated.

**If something can flex, flex the scope, not the price:**

```
"I can't move on price, but I can include [specific thing] at no charge so you
get a clear first result."
```

**Never discount to match a competitor.** You'll lose margin and confirm your
price was a negotiation rather than a valuation.

**Real urgency only.** Time-limited and stock-limited offers must be true.
Manufactured scarcity is deceptive practice — see `outbound-compliance.md`.

**If you're consistently losing on price,** the fix is positioning, not margin.

## Phrasing

**Investment, not cost.** "The investment is $[n]" frames return; "it costs $[n]"
frames loss. Accurate, not spin.

**Because.** Follow the number with a reason: "$[n] because it includes…". It
moves the conversation from "how much" to "what for" and pre-empts the obvious
challenge.

**Certainty.** "The investment is $[n]" over "it might be around $[n]".

**Attach to outcome, not effort.** Not "this costs $[n] because it takes our
team a long time" — that invites argument about the hours. Attach it to what they
get.

**Close on a question, not silence.** On a call, silence after a price is
uncomfortable for both parties. "Does that work within what you had in mind, or
would you like me to look at adjusting the scope?" keeps the call alive and
offers an off-ramp that isn't a discount.

**Currency and magnitude first, digits last.** "Six hundred pounds" rather than
"$600". "About two thousand a month" rather than "$2,000/month".
